The question of who should bear the primary responsibility for the care of the elderly—adult children, the government, or the elderly themselves—is complex and varies across moral theories. Many cultures emphasize filial obligation. A common belief suggests that adult children are obliged to take care of their elderly parents because the parents sacrifices their time and money in the process which they educate and nuture their children. Hence, adult children owe their parents and have the moral duty to repay the debts. However, this traditional thought is criticized by scholars. In the context of "owing debts", once the debts are repayed, the debtors are discharged from further repayment, which is not the case in our perception of filial obligation, where the "repayment" is not clear defined. Critics further argue that the parental care were not resquested by the children, thus children are neither owing their parents' debt nor responsible for paying their parents back[@English1992]. If children do not owe their parent a debt, is it justified for them to not care their aging parents? Some might suggests that the parent-child relationship itself has intrinsic value, obligating both sides to maintain it. In this sense, children do not take care of parents because of owing their parents. Instead, taking care of parents bring children a special good that can obtained by other ways[@stuifbergen2011filial].

The role played by the government in elderly care arise from the social contract theory, which suggests that individuals surrender some freedoms to the government in exchange for protection and services. The elderly is a group of vulnerable population and their well-being is heavily relied on the community services and public healthcare. The opinion on thw stretagies of elderly care and the responsiblity by the government can be guided by the justice principles, which involve both legal aspects and political goals, especially the fair distribution of resources[@dean2004ethics]. Regarding the unequal social and economic status among the older population, the state should actively invest in institutional care systems and support unpaid caregivers through policies. As such investment requires considerable expenses, the government would implement policies such as progressive taxation which redistribute the property from the wealth. Libertarian would oppose the intervention by the government as the redistribution violates the property rights of the individuals. They claim that the role of the state should be minial and consider free market is the most efficient way of distributing property. Hence, libertarian will suggest that the problem of elderly care would be solved as there are private companies providing caregivers and nursing home. The opponents will claim that the absence of regulation to the markets would exacerbate poverty and discourage the poor from accesing adequate healthcare services[@wolff2023introduction].

The idea that individuals should take responsibility for their well-being is approved by some ethical traditions. In Kantian ethics, people are treated as an end rather than a mere means beause they are rational agents, which are capable of making decision by reason. Self-neglecting may lead to the inability for reasoning and violate our moral duty. Aristotle's virtue ethics also suggests that self-discipline is a character which make someone a virtuous person. Thus, neglecting oneself could lead to a failure to be virtuous[@rachels2012elements]. Despite some may suggest that caring for oneself are moral duty, it is unrealistic to expect elderly to bear full responsibility for their own care. Age-related illnesses like dementia or mobility issues can severely limit their ability to manage daily life. Therefore, the duty of the government and the adult children is essential.
